[Last week I wrote about running my actual GitHub Actions workflows on my own
Mac](/blog/free-ci-on-my-own-mac) as self-hosted runners — same YAML, same
merge gate, ~$500/month down to about $0. The moment you internalize that
GitHub doesn't meter Actions minutes on hardware you own, a bigger idea shows
up almost on its own:

Every software company hands its engineers a laptop that's several thousand
dollars of compute. Most of the day most of those machines are 90% idle —
reading Slack, waiting on a build, sitting locked at lunch. **What if a little
daemon lived on all of them, polled GitHub for queued Actions jobs, and ran
them whenever the machine had spare cycles?** Jobs run primarily on employee
hardware that would otherwise be wasted; when no idle capacity is around, they
fall back to the paid GitHub-hosted runners the company already budgets for. No
speed loss if you want none, and the CI invoice drops toward zero.

I like this idea. I want it to be true. So I went looking for who's already
built it and why it isn't everywhere — and the honest answer is more
interesting than a straight pitch would be.

## The idea already has a name — and it's older than I am

Harvesting idle cycles off machines people already own is one of the oldest
ideas in distributed systems. It's called **cycle scavenging** (or cycle
stealing), and the canonical system is **Condor** — now
[HTCondor](https://htcondor.org/description.html) — built at the University of
Wisconsin–Madison starting in **1984**. Condor's whole job was to notice which
workstations on campus were idle and run batch work on them. It even solved the
problems you'd immediately worry about: it watched the keyboard and mouse and
only used a machine while its owner was away, and when the owner came back it
would **checkpoint** the running job and **migrate** it to another idle box so
the reclaimed work wasn't lost. Forty years later it's still running science on
scavenged cores.

Then the internet scaled it up. **SETI@home** and **Folding@home** (both 1999),
generalized by Berkeley's **BOINC** (2002), turned millions of idle home PCs
into supercomputers for radio-signal and protein-folding work. "There is idle
compute everywhere; go use it" is not a new thought. It's a well-explored one
with a decades-long research literature behind it.

So the instinct is sound and ancient. The interesting question isn't _has anyone
thought of this_ — it's _why did the whole industry walk away from it_.

## Why the cloud won anyway

Cycle scavenging thrived on a specific kind of work: embarrassingly parallel,
checkpointable, latency-insensitive batch jobs. Analyze a chunk of sky; if the
machine dies mid-chunk, hand the chunk to someone else; nobody's waiting on any
single result. Scavenged desktops are heterogeneous, intermittently available,
and messy — and for that workload none of it mattered.

The cloud won by inverting the trade. Data centers gave you **homogeneous,
elastic, on-demand, reproducible** compute behind an API. You stopped depending
on whether someone else's machine happened to be on, idle, and networked. You
traded "free but unreliable and inconsistent" for "paid but uniform and always
there." Every serious CI system is built on the second half of that trade —
which is exactly the half the every-laptop idea proposes to give back.

That's the tension. And CI turns out to be close to the _worst_ possible fit for
scavenged compute, for reasons that become obvious the moment you scale my setup
from one person to a company.

## Why it works for me

My solo version isn't clever. It works because a solo, pre-launch,
agent-operated repo happens to satisfy four conditions that CI quietly demands:

1. **I trust every line of code that runs.** No fork PRs, no outside
   contributors, no untrusted workflows. The runner executes my code on my
   machine.
2. **I have exactly one environment.** One Mac, one toolchain. Nothing to keep
   consistent across a fleet.
3. **Nobody expensive is waiting.** [The coding agent pushes, waits, reads what's
   red, and fixes it](/blog/killing-ci-for-local-only) — unattended. Latency
   doesn't cost me anything because I'm not standing there.
4. **My compute bill genuinely exceeded my labor bill.** Pre-launch, zero users,
   the CI meter was the biggest line item I had.

Scale that to a company and it inverts all four.

## Why a company has none of them

**Trust is the killer.** A self-hosted runner is a persistent code-execution
endpoint, and GitHub's own docs are blunt that self-hosted runners
[should almost never be used with public repositories](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/security/secure-use):
a fork PR can rewrite the workflow and run arbitrary code on your runner, which
isn't sandboxed and can stay compromised between jobs. Now put that endpoint on
a laptop that also holds SSO cookies, cloud credentials, SSH keys, and the
source tree. Security teams have already documented attackers
[using self-hosted runners as backdoors](https://www.sysdig.com/blog/how-threat-actors-are-using-self-hosted-github-actions-runners-as-backdoors).
A daemon on _every_ laptop that pulls and executes jobs isn't a cost
optimization; it's company-wide remote code execution with a fallback plan.
That's the opposite of what you want on the machines holding your crown jewels.

**Reproducibility goes with it.** A cloud runner is a clean VM per job. Employee
laptops are long-lived, dirty, and heterogeneous — Intel and Apple Silicon,
different OS versions, different installed tools, leftover state from actual
work. "Works on my machine" stops being a joke and becomes your CI policy.
You can claw reproducibility back with containers, but that's exactly the
overhead the pitch was trying to avoid, and it's painful-to-impossible for the
macOS/iOS builds where the idea is most tempting.

**Latency stops being free, and availability turns adversarial.** My agent eats
the wait; a company _pays engineers to wait_. Shaving a $500 bill is noise next
to a team idling on a build. And the pool you'd schedule against is a coin flip:
laptops sleep, lids close, machines roam onto VPN and behind NAT, people walk
out of the building. Worse, availability is anti-correlated with demand —
everyone pushes before standup, which is precisely when every laptop is busy
being _worked on_. The one machine you can safely scavenge is the one nobody's
using, which for a working employee mostly means nights and weekends — a batch
window, not a live-CI fleet.

**And the economics usually point the other way.** For a funded company the CI
invoice is a rounding error against salaries; the real cost is engineers waiting
on slow, flaky builds. Which is why the actual market moved in the _opposite_
direction: [Depot](https://depot.dev), Blacksmith, WarpBuild, Namespace and a
dozen others sell drop-in runners that are **faster** (and often cheaper) than
GitHub's, competing on caching and raw CPU — not on scavenging anyone's laptop.
Even DHH's 37signals, when they
[moved CI back onto developer machines](https://world.hey.com/dhh/we-re-moving-continuous-integration-back-to-developer-machines-3ac6c611),
did it for _speed_ — their beefy dev boxes beat the cloud run — and explicitly
on the committer's _own_ machine, not by harvesting idle cycles from everyone
else's.

## Where the idea actually has teeth: macOS

There's one place the numbers genuinely bite. GitHub's macOS runners cost
roughly **ten times** its Linux ones, because Apple's licensing forces real Mac
hardware in the loop. A company full of MacBooks is sitting on a pile of
expensive, idle Apple Silicon — and self-hosting Mac CI is
[documented saving thousands a month](https://jeffverkoeyen.com/blog/2025/10/17/SelfHostingMacMinis/).

But notice how that case gets solved in practice: **dedicated Mac minis in a
closet**, or a self-hosted runner pool, not a daemon riding everyone's working
laptop. That's the tell. The savings come from owning always-on, trusted,
uniform hardware — which is a rack of minis, not the machine an engineer is
mid-sentence on. It's also, quietly, what I actually did: I run CI on a Mac I've
_dedicated_ to it, not the one I'm typing this on. GitHub already ships the
grown-up version of this —
[Actions Runner Controller](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/how-tos/manage-runners/self-hosted-runners/add-runners),
auto-scaling ephemeral runners on your own boxes, with hosted runners as
overflow. The pattern in the pitch is real and supported. The part nobody ships
is putting it on the laptops people work on.

Even the closest modern restatement dodges the hard part. HashiCorp's engineers
recently described
[harvesting idle office compute](https://hashicorpengineering.substack.com/p/harvesting-idle-office-compute-with)
by turning employee workstations into an overnight cluster — but for _batch_
jobs, in a fixed nighttime window, drained before 8 a.m., and they still flag
the blast-radius risk of running arbitrary jobs across the fleet. Overnight
batch on trusted internal work is Condor 1984 with better tooling. Live,
latency-sensitive CI carrying untrusted PRs on machines full of secrets is a
different animal.

## The honest version

I went looking for the exact thing — a daemon opportunistically stealing idle
cycles from _active employee laptops_ to run GitHub Actions, with paid fallback
— and as far as I can tell nobody ships it. Not because it's unthought-of: it's
just cycle scavenging (1984) plus "CI on dev machines" (DHH) plus "harvest the
office" (HashiCorp), recombined. It's unshipped because assembling those three
re-introduces every failure mode my little setup happens to sidestep. Trust,
reproducibility, latency, availability, and the actual math all break the moment
you go from _my code on my machine_ to _everyone's code on everyone's machine_.

So the reframe I'd offer past-me, mid-daydream: the reason my $500 went to zero
isn't that I found free compute lying around that companies are too dim to use.
It's that a solo pre-launch repo dissolves the four things that make idle-cycle
CI hard — and a company is defined by having all four back. The right-sized
version of the idea already exists and companies already run it: **dedicated,
trusted, self-hosted runner pools, with hosted runners as overflow.** That's the
part worth copying. The romantic part — a build quietly humming on every
coworker's laptop while they work — is romantic for a reason.
