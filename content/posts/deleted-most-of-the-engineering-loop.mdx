I've written about this loop twice already.

In [June](/blog/shipyard-and-lightwork) I wrote about building shipyard — an
autonomous engineering loop on Claude Code — and what it felt like to wake up
to 116 merged pull requests. In [July](/blog/everyone-built-the-same-loop) I
wrote that the industry had converged on the same architecture and that "a
meaningful chunk of shipyard's plumbing quietly became a native feature I no
longer needed." I ended that post saying the trick is to keep asking the
machine what it's missing.

So I asked again. This time the answer was: _most of me._

## The number I didn't want

I pointed a session at my own repo and asked it to measure, not estimate.

shipyard was 9.9 MB across 43 agents, 73 command files, 31 skills and 258
scripts — about 137,000 lines. The prompt corpus alone came to roughly 700,000
tokens.

Then the part that actually settled it:

- **176 of 258 scripts — 68% — were tests of shipyard itself.** 66,784 lines of
  self-test against 36,333 lines of tool. **1.8 lines of test per line of
  thing-being-tested.**
- **119 of those tests had no executable at all.** They asserted on prompt
  *markdown*: that two hand-copied versions of the same dispatch prompt still
  matched, that 205 deep links into a 531 KB rationale file still resolved,
  that a phase file hadn't exceeded its token budget.
- **62 of 80 top-level scripts — 27,722 of 36,333 lines, 76% — now have a
  first-party equivalent.** Worktree provisioning and reaping (7,538 lines)
  is `Agent(isolation: "worktree")`. Session state and a status dashboard
  (4,637 lines) is `TaskCreate`/`TaskUpdate`. CI polling and a `gh` response
  cache (4,394 lines) is `Monitor` plus the GitHub MCP. Adversarial
  verification before auto-merge is `/code-review ultra`.

And the figure that reframed the whole thing: shipyard's own repo has absorbed
**793 issues and 1,270 commits**. It delivered 1,788 merged PRs to my app in
that time — so I filed roughly **0.44 shipyard issues per pull request
shipped**. Nearly half a unit of harness maintenance for every unit of work.

That ratio was fine when the harness was the only way to get the work done. It
stopped being fine the moment it wasn't.

## Uncle Bob got there five weeks after I wrote about it

On **4 August 2026**, Robert C. Martin
[described](https://x.com/unclebobmartin/status/2084638878548304046) building
an orchestration harness for "a squad of agents" — analysts, reviewers,
gherkin authors, QA authors, implementers, cleaners, code reviewers, hardeners,
architects — driven by a static finite state machine, with a simulator and
Monte Carlo testing over it. He ended the post: _"Don't tell me software
engineering is dead."_

On **11 September**, five weeks later, he
[posted this](https://x.com/unclebobmartin/status/2098432570887217520):

> I've spend the last several weeks working on a harness that tightly
> constrains the agents to work the way that I want them to work. I set up all
> kinds of gates, and tests, and tools, and protocols, and …
>
> And while I was heads-down getting that to work, the agents got a LOT better.
> So much so that when I came up for air, the need for my harness was obviated.
> Indeed, the need for _any_ but the most liberal of harnesses may be obviated.

Three days later: _"Since I stopped using my harness, my token consumption has
fallen by a huge factor. That harness was massively inefficient."_ And a week
after that, the thesis in one line: _"the models have gotten good enough that
strict deterministic harnesses are suboptimal."_

Martin Fowler
[wrote it up](https://martinfowler.com/fragments/2026-09-16.html) on 16
September, which is how most people saw it.

I'm noting the dates because the chronology surprised me, not to claim
anything by it. My convergence post was 22 July. His reversal was 11
September. We were looking at the same thing from opposite ends — he built the
harness and watched it become unnecessary; I built one, watched the industry
rebuild it natively, and took another two months to act. Neither of us was
early. The models were just moving faster than either of us was typing.

## The people who ship the models say it louder

This is the part that should carry more weight than any of us with a side
project.

Anthropic's Claude Code team [cut about 80% of the Claude Code system
prompt](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jul/21/cat-and-thariq/). Thariq
Shihipar: _"we were over-constraining Claude… removing examples was extremely
helpful."_ And Cat Wu, with the detail that makes it airtight:

> We actually have a **different system prompt per model** now, for this very
> reason. It's only our most frontier models that have this 80% token
> decrease — the older models still have the full system prompt.

Scaffolding, versioned by model vintage, by the people who wrote it.

Boris Cherny, who created Claude Code, at YC Startup School: _"We unship tools
all the time. We delete code in the harness all the time. If you look at the
code that's in the Claude Code harness today, almost all of it is about safety
and permissions and static analysis."_ His advice to everyone else was to
_"every six months, delete your [CLAUDE.md], delete your skills, delete your
hooks"_ and see what the model does without them.

Han Lee has the sentence I wish I'd written: **"The model ate the harness."**
His warning is the one I'd already walked into — that teams who don't treat
the harness as a 90-day artifact end up _"building load-bearing scaffolding
around a model that is about to outgrow it."_

Browser Use published the same reversal with receipts: a January post arguing
frameworks are the bitter lesson in miniature, then an April post that opens
_"Turns out we were wrong"_ about their own previous conclusion, ending at
~600 lines total. And Cognition has the cleanest single example I've seen: in
2024, models were bad at editing code, so everyone — including them — ran a
second "edit apply model" to turn a large model's markdown description of a
change into an actual file edit. That entire category of scaffolding existed
because of one weakness. Today one model does both in one action. The
scaffolding didn't get refactored. It evaporated.

## What I actually cut, and what I kept

Here's where I part ways with the tidy version of this story.

I did **not** delete the loop. `/do-work` is the command I use most; deleting
it to make a cleaner narrative would have been a worse decision dressed as a
braver one. What I deleted is the *substrate* — the implementations shipyard
wrote before the platform had them.

**Gone:** an entire alternate dispatch substrate (1.9 MB of spec and a
generated orchestration script) that existed only because its primitive had no
worktree isolation — which the `Agent` tool now provides for free. Both
"rationale" files, 544 KB between them, with a dedicated link checker to keep
their 205 anchors valid. The prompt-parity checkers whose only job was
verifying that shipyard's copies of its own text still agreed with each other.
A command built to detect context bloat in runtime-loaded markdown — i.e. to
fight my own prompt corpus, which I'm now deleting directly. And an
issue-refiner that classified and rewrote raw feedback in a pass before
dispatch, which a current model now does inline while reading the issue.

**Kept, and not reluctantly:**

- **19 auditors.** They filed 549 issues against my app over its life. They're
  the half that produces *new information* rather than throughput, and they're
  stateless one-shot prompts with no orchestrator underneath.
- **Six `refuse-*` safety hooks.** Each guards an externally irreversible
  action and each exists because something actually went wrong: a worker
  minting a production service-account key, a `pkill -f "playwright test"`
  that killed two in-flight CI shards on a shared host, a worker deleting a
  Vercel production environment variable while its own fix PR was still open.

A smarter model makes that class of mistake less often. It does not make the
mistake cost less. Unbounded downside doesn't care about your model version.

## The counter-argument, which is better than I'd like

Someone pre-registered my exact hypothesis and it came out the other way.

["Scaffold Effects on GAIA: A Controlled
Comparison"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08529) held tasks fixed across five
models and three providers and varied only the scaffold. Scaffold choice moved
measured accuracy by up to **28 percentage points** within a single model. And:

> The pre-registered prediction that more capable models would be less
> scaffold-sensitive is **rejected in direction**… the most capable Anthropic
> model gains the most from structured scaffolds at the harder level… the
> elicitation gap is not guaranteed to shrink as models improve.

I don't think that kills the argument, but it sharpens what the argument can
honestly be. "Structure stops mattering" is not supported. "*This particular*
structure, written for a 2026-05 model, stopped paying for itself" is what I
measured, and it's a much smaller claim.

Addy Osmani — arguing the pro-harness side — put the reconciliation better
than I can:

> You only add constraints when you've seen a real failure. You only remove
> them when a capable model has made them redundant.

Both halves of that sentence are the job. I spent four months doing the first
half diligently and the second half not at all.

## The line that actually holds

Look at what survived each of these purges.

Uncle Bob kept his unit tests, his CRAP metric, his mutation testing — and
dropped the orchestration. Boris Cherny says what's left in the Claude Code
harness is "almost all about safety and permissions and static analysis." I
kept auditors that find problems and hooks that refuse irreversible actions,
and deleted the machinery that told a model what order to do things in.

**Deterministic verification survives. Imperative orchestration of the model
does not.**

That isn't a compromise between the two camps. It's what both camps did when
they actually cut something.

## The part that proves it

While I was deleting the orchestration layer, the verification layer caught me
four times.

I removed a section of spec that looked substrate-specific; three test suites
failed because one sentence in it — "do not `git worktree add` again and do
not create a new branch" — was a universal rule about resuming a stalled
worker, not a property of the thing I was deleting. A link checker found ten
dead references the deletion created. A reference resolver found four command
names that no longer resolved, and its own error message told me not to widen
it to make the failure go away. `shellcheck` found a helper function I'd
orphaned.

And the one that actually matters: I deleted a command that wrote provenance
markers onto issues, then deleted the code that *read* those markers — and
broke the human-review queue for every issue still carrying one. The markers
don't disappear when the writer does. Two red CI runs told me so.

Every one of those was a deterministic gate catching a judgment error, on the
specific change whose entire thesis is that judgment has gotten good enough to
need fewer gates. I don't think that's ironic. I think it's the finding. The
gates weren't guarding against a dumb model. They were guarding against
*confident, plausible, wrong* — which is exactly what better models produce
more of, not less.

## Where it landed

Three stages in, the picture looks like this: 9.9 MB heading for roughly 2.5,
with `/do-work` working exactly as it did. But the honest progress number is
small so far — about 9.9 MB down to 9.1. **92% of the remaining reduction sits
in a single stage**: the orchestrator spec, its session-state plumbing, and the
3 MB of self-tests that exist only to guard a prompt corpus that's shrinking.
That one comes out as a piece or not at all, which is exactly why it's last.

My July post said the plumbing converged and the judgment didn't. I still
think that's right. What I'd add now is that "judgment" turned out to include
knowing when your own scaffolding has become the thing in the way — and that
this is a measurement, not a feeling. I had the feeling in July. It took until
September to run the numbers, and the numbers were worse than the feeling.

The map was still worth having. I just should have looked at it sooner.
